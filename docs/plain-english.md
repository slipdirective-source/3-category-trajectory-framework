# On Trajectory

**By Caleb Aaron Meadows** · CC BY 4.0 · An introduction to the 3-Category Trajectory Framework (rev. 7)

## The one idea

You can't understand a system from a snapshot. You understand it by watching what it *does* — its trajectory. A photo of a car tells you nothing about the driver; watching the drive tells you everything.

That's the whole framework: **things are known by the trajectory they produce, not by static description.**

## The three stages

Every run of anything — a program, a project, a decision, a habit — moves through three stages.

### 1. The Starting Line

Before anything moves, four things have to be settled:

- **The non-negotiables** — what's forbidden, and the rule that when something goes wrong, everything stops safely (not hopefully).
- **The starting conditions** — what you're working with, what tools you have, and crucially, what you're *allowed* to do. Your envelope.
- **Who's in charge** — the identity everything gets checked against. Call it the anchor. If the anchor itself changes, that's a formal handoff: the new one has to prove it follows from the old, or it's treated as a stranger at the door.
- **The door check** — if what you're handed doesn't check out against who's in charge, you don't start. Full stop. Nothing enters stage 2.

### 2. The Drive

The system runs. Three things happen continuously:

- Every move is checked against the rules *as it happens*.
- Every move is written down, in a record nobody can secretly edit.
- The system can adjust *within* the rules — slow down, reroute, pull over, stop. But it can never quietly grant itself *more* power than it was given. If it tries — through mission creep, unverified inputs, borrowed permissions, or loopholes that escalate privilege — everything stops.

### 3. The Arrival

The run finishes:

- The result is checked against the rules. For systems that don't give the same answer twice — like AI — the output has to carry proof of where it came from: its inputs, its anchor, its record.
- The full record is sealed and packed up — so the next run can start from it, honestly.
- Anything coming in from outside gets verified at the door, not trusted on arrival.

Then it's back to the Starting Line for the next run. Start → Drive → Arrive → Start again.

## The rules about the rules

Rules come in two kinds.

**Foundation rules** — change one and you're playing a different game entirely:

- Don't contradict yourself.
- When in doubt, stop safely. (A system that keeps going when confused isn't this framework.)
- Write everything down. (Without the record, the whole idea collapses.)
- Someone has to be in charge. (Checking has to end somewhere.)
- That someone can't be the one being checked. (The person taking the test can't rewrite it mid-exam.)
- Stay inside the power you were granted. (If your abilities quietly grow beyond what was allowed, that's a breach, and everything stops.)

**Settings** — limits, thresholds, budgets. These *can* change, but only *between* runs, never mid-drive. Mid-drive, you're only ever allowed to tighten, never loosen.

One catch: some things never stop — running agents, trading books, ongoing operations. There is no automatic "between runs" for those; you have to build one by deliberately stopping, changing the settings, and restarting.

And one more: **a rulebook its author can rewrite at will is a diary, not a rulebook.** Real rules have to resist the person they bind — through code that can't be edited mid-run, time-locks, outside witnesses, or some equivalent. Otherwise it's just intentions with formatting.

One boundary: this checks the rules you actually declared — not the ones you meant but never wrote down. If the rule that mattered was never declared, that's a failure at the Starting Line, and the audit names the omission.

## The ideas underneath

**"Nothing can be trusted — only observed. If it's trusted, it's dead."**

A parked car can be trusted. A moving car has to be watched. That's why this framework watches continuously instead of certifying once and looking away. Trust is what you say about something *after* it stops moving.

**"You can't revoke authorship of a consequence just because it didn't go the way you imagined."**

If you set it in motion, you own what it does — including the failures. The framework's record exists so authorship can't be laundered after the fact. A bad outcome is a broken trajectory, not a disappeared author.

**The quiet failures are the dangerous ones.**

The worst breakdowns aren't rule-breaking — they're the system doing *more* than it was allowed while technically following every rule. A budget followed to the letter while the spending power quietly doubles. That's why the framework watches the *envelope* — the granted power — not just the rules.

## What it's for

Anywhere something moves from start to finish and you need to know it stayed honest:

- **Software** — especially AI systems that act on their own
- **Organizations** — governance, risk, and compliance that actually holds
- **Personal** — habits, commitments, the trajectory of your own decisions. Here you're both the checker and the checked, so the rules have to be set down *before* the drive, in a form your later self can't quietly edit — because you're the one most tempted to rewrite them. A self-check counts only as an attestation: honest if it names its own blind spots, never a clean bill of health.
